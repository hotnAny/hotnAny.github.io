This is a book that documents Simon's school of work on computational scientific discovery. Historically speaking, we should be impressed by how Simon and colleagues started thinking about AI that conducts scientific research at such an early time—a mission that is still ongoing today. On the other hand, I have always had reservations about this school of work.

The fundamental assumption is that scientific research is basically a search problem. But unlike searching for a number in a data structure, there is no prescribed algorithm; one has to rely on heuristics to guide where to search—thus the crux, "heuristic search".

My problem with this assumption is that search is at best a subtask of the general scientific discovery process, typically at the stage when you have conducted some studies or experiments and have obtained some data. For example, using heuristic search, you can find a model that explains why your data are the way they are.

But so much more needs to happen before you have the data. How about problem definition? Hypothesis generation? Hypothesis development (e.g., breaking down a hierarchy of hypotheses)? Experimental design?

Further, search seems to me to imply some extent of (strategically) enumerating a finite set of possibilities. Yet one example of an activity in scientific discovery that is not necessarily search is abductive reasoning, or inference to the best explanation, which is a key approach for coming up with hypotheses.

Indeed, if you look at the book's case studies of heuristic search, they are almost indistinguishable from what modern data science is doing: given some data, trying to answer a question. It is still part of the scientific discovery process—just not all of it.
